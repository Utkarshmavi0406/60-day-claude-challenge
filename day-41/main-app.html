The Binomial Distribution

Foundational · Discrete Distributions

What is a probability distribution?

A random variable is a numeric outcome of something uncertain — the number of heads in 10 coin flips, the number of calls a helpdesk gets in an hour, someone's height. A probability distribution is simply the complete list of every possible value that random variable can take, paired with how likely each value is.

Distributions come in two families. Discrete distributions describe random variables that take countable values — 0, 1, 2, 3 defective items, never 2.5. Continuous distributions describe values that can fall anywhere on a range — height, temperature, time. Discrete distributions use a PMF (probability mass function); continuous ones use a PDF (probability density function). The Binomial and Poisson distributions you'll learn first are both discrete; the Normal distribution later in this course is continuous.

The Binomial setup: counting successes

The Binomial distribution answers one very specific question: "If I repeat the same yes/no experiment n times, how many times will I get a 'yes'?" It applies whenever four conditions hold:

  • Fixed number of trials (n) — you decide in advance how many times you'll repeat the experiment
  • Two possible outcomes per trial — success or failure (a "Bernoulli trial")
  • Constant probability of success (p) — it doesn't change from trial to trial
  • Independent trials — one outcome doesn't affect another
Think of the Binomial distribution as a bag of coin flips. Flip a coin 10 times (n=10), and count how many land heads. You could get 0 heads, or all 10 — the Binomial distribution tells you exactly how likely each of those 11 outcomes is, before you even flip.
Binomial Probability Mass Function P(X = k) = C(n, k) · pk · (1−p)n−k

Here C(n, k) is "n choose k" — the number of different ways to arrange k successes among n trials — multiplied by the probability of any one specific arrangement of k successes and (n−k) failures.

Mean & Variance Mean = n·p   |   Variance = n·p·(1−p)

Worked example

Exercise 1.1

A factory's quality control process finds that 5% of light bulbs produced are defective (p = 0.05). A random sample of 20 bulbs is tested (n = 20). What is the probability that exactly 2 bulbs are defective?

P(X=2) = C(20,2) · (0.05)2 · (0.95)18

C(20,2) = 190. (0.05)² = 0.0025. (0.95)18 ≈ 0.3972

P(X=2) = 190 × 0.0025 × 0.3972 ≈ 0.1887, or about 18.9%.

Exercise 1.2 — Try it in the simulator above

Set n = 20 and p = 5 (representing 0.05) in the simulator. Read off the bar at k = 2. Does the height roughly match the ~18.9% you calculated? This is exactly how you'll sanity-check hand calculations against the visual distribution in practice.

"Independent trials" doesn't mean the trials have to be physically different actions — it means the outcome of one trial gives you no information about another. Drawing cards without replacement from a deck is not binomial (each draw changes the odds for the next), but drawing with replacement, or flipping a coin repeatedly, is.
The Binomial distribution is fully described by just two numbers: n and p. If you can identify "how many trials" and "probability of success per trial" in a word problem, you can compute any probability from the PMF formula.

The Poisson Distribution

Modeling Rare Events · Discrete Distributions

From counting successes to counting events

The Binomial distribution needed a fixed number of trials, n. But some things don't come in trials at all — you don't "attempt" a customer arrival 1,000 times and count successes. Instead, events just happen, scattered across a continuous stretch of time or space: emails arriving, potholes on a stretch of road, typos on a page.

The Poisson distribution models the count of independent events that occur at a known average rate over a fixed interval, when the events could in principle happen any number of times.

Picture raindrops falling on a sidewalk square. You know the average rate — say 4 drops per square per minute — but you can't say "how many trials" there were; rain doesn't have trials. The Poisson distribution tells you the probability of counting exactly 0, 1, 2, 10 drops in that square in a given minute.

The Poisson formula

Poisson Probability Mass Function P(X = k) = (λk · e−λ) / k!

λ (lambda) is the single parameter — the average number of events per interval. Unlike the Binomial's two parameters (n and p), Poisson needs only λ, because it's really describing the limit of a Binomial where n grows huge and p shrinks tiny, while n·p stays fixed at λ.

Mean & Variance Mean = λ   |   Variance = λ

That's a distinctive Poisson fingerprint: mean and variance are always equal. If you're looking at count data and the sample variance is roughly equal to the sample mean, Poisson is a strong candidate model.

How Poisson connects back to Binomial

Imagine slicing an hour into 3,600 one-second intervals and asking "did a call arrive in this second?" as a yes/no Binomial trial with tiny p. As you slice time into smaller and smaller intervals (n → ∞) while keeping the average rate n·p = λ constant, the Binomial distribution's shape converges exactly to the Poisson distribution. Poisson is what Binomial becomes when trials become too frequent and individually improbable to count.

Worked example

Exercise 2.1

A support inbox receives an average of 6 emails per hour (λ = 6). What is the probability that exactly 4 emails arrive in a given hour?

P(X=4) = (64 · e−6) / 4!

64 = 1296. e−6 ≈ 0.002479. 4! = 24

P(X=4) = (1296 × 0.002479) / 24 ≈ 0.1339, or about 13.4%.

λ doesn't have to be a whole number — "an average of 2.3 defects per meter" is a perfectly valid Poisson rate, even though you'll never observe exactly 2.3 defects in any single meter. The mean of a distribution describing counts can still be fractional; only the outcomes k are whole numbers.
Reach for Poisson when you're counting how many times something happens in a fixed window of time, space, or another continuous measure, and events happen independently at a roughly constant average rate.

The Normal Distribution

Continuous Distributions · The Bell Curve

A different kind of distribution

Binomial and Poisson both describe counts — whole numbers you could list out one by one. The Normal distribution (also called the Gaussian distribution) is continuous: it describes measurements that can take any value on a range, like height, blood pressure, or measurement error. Because there are infinitely many possible values, we can't ask "what's the probability of exactly 5.7?" — that probability is technically zero. Instead, we ask about probability over a range, represented as the area under the curve.

Think of the Normal distribution as sand poured through a funnel onto a table. Most of it piles up directly under the funnel's center — that's the mean. Less and less lands as you move away in either direction, and the pile is symmetric left and right. That pile shape is the bell curve.

The Normal formula

Normal Probability Density Function f(x) = ( 1 / (σ√(2π)) ) · e−(x−μ)²/(2σ²)

Two parameters fully describe it: μ (mu), the mean — where the peak sits — and σ (sigma), the standard deviation — how spread out it is. You will rarely compute f(x) by hand; what matters far more in practice is the empirical rule and z-scores, both below.

The empirical rule (68–95–99.7)

For any Normal distribution, no matter its mean or spread:

  • About 68% of values fall within 1 standard deviation of the mean
  • About 95% fall within 2 standard deviations
  • About 99.7% fall within 3 standard deviations

This single rule is why the Normal distribution is so useful for quick judgment calls: if you know the mean and standard deviation of something normally distributed, you already know roughly where almost all the data lives.

Try it yourself: live Normal curve + empirical rule

Normal PDF with 68–95–99.7 shading

Z-scores: putting any value on a common scale

Z-Score z = (x − μ) / σ

A z-score tells you how many standard deviations a value sits from the mean. It turns any Normal distribution into the same "standard" reference frame, which is why z-tables exist as one universal table instead of one per possible μ and σ.

Worked example

Exercise 3.1

Adult male heights are approximately Normal with μ = 70 inches and σ = 3 inches. What is the z-score for a height of 76 inches, and roughly what percentile does that correspond to?

z = (76 − 70) / 3 = 2.00

A z-score of 2.00 means this height is exactly 2 standard deviations above the mean. By the empirical rule, ~95% of values lie within ±2σ, so ~2.5% lie above +2σ — this height is roughly at the 97.5th percentile.

When can Binomial be approximated by Normal?

As n grows large in a Binomial distribution, its shape becomes smoother and increasingly bell-like — you may have noticed this already in the Module 1 simulator with large n near p = 0.5. A common rule of thumb: if n·p ≥ 5 and n·(1−p) ≥ 5, the Binomial(n, p) can be reasonably approximated by a Normal distribution with μ = n·p and σ = √(n·p·(1−p)). This is a preview of the Central Limit Theorem you'll see formally in Module 4.

"Normal" doesn't mean "typical" or "the default distribution real-world data follows." Many real quantities — income, city population, word frequency — are heavily skewed, not Normal. The name is historical, not a claim that it's the natural baseline for everything.
The Normal distribution is defined entirely by μ and σ. Once you know those two numbers, the empirical rule and z-scores let you reason about probabilities without ever touching the density formula directly.

The Central Limit Theorem: why Normal shows up everywhere

The Central Limit Theorem (CLT) is the reason the Normal distribution keeps reappearing throughout this course, even in Modules about entirely different distributions. It states: if you take many independent samples from almost any distribution and look at the distribution of their averages (or sums), that distribution of averages approaches Normal as the sample size grows — regardless of the shape of the original distribution.

Imagine rolling a single six-sided die — the outcomes 1 through 6 are equally likely, a flat distribution, not bell-shaped at all. Now roll 5 dice and record the average. Do that thousands of times. The average is rarely exactly 1 or exactly 6 — most averages cluster near 3.5, tapering off symmetrically. That clustering-and-tapering is the Normal shape emerging from a distribution that wasn't Normal at all.

CLT Simulator — average of rolling N dice, repeated 2,000 times

N = 1: this is just a single die roll — flat, not Normal yet.
Slide N up from 1 to 12 and watch a flat, uniform distribution morph into a bell curve. This is the same mathematical force that lets a Normal distribution approximate a Binomial with large n, and it's why so many real-world measurements — which are often the sum of many small independent effects — end up looking roughly Normal even when no one designed them to.

Choosing the right distribution — practice scenarios

Exercise 4.1

Scenario A: You flip a fair coin 15 times and count how many land heads.

Binomial(n=15, p=0.5) — fixed number of trials, two outcomes per trial, constant probability, independent flips. Textbook Binomial setup.

Exercise 4.2

Scenario B: A website logs an average of 12 sign-ups per day and you want the distribution of daily sign-up counts.

Poisson(λ=12) — no fixed "number of trials," just a count of independent events (sign-ups) over a fixed interval (a day) at a known average rate.

Exercise 4.3

Scenario C: You measure the exact weight (in grams) of 500 apples from an orchard.

Normal(μ, σ) — weight is a continuous measurement, not a count, and biological measurements like this commonly cluster symmetrically around an average due to many small independent growth factors (a CLT-flavored intuition).

Choosing a distribution isn't about which formula looks most convenient — it's about matching the structure of the real process (fixed trials vs. rate-based counts vs. continuous measurement) to the distribution's assumptions. When the structure doesn't fit, the numbers you get out are meaningless even if the arithmetic is correct.
Binomial counts successes out of a known number of attempts. Poisson counts events at a known rate with no fixed number of attempts. Normal describes continuous measurements — and, via the Central Limit Theorem, describes the behavior of averages and sums of almost anything, including Binomial and Poisson themselves at large scale.

Final Practical Challenge

Apply all three distributions in one scenario

You're a data analyst at a regional call center. Work through all three parts below — each uses a different distribution from this course.

Part A — Poisson

The call center receives an average of 8 calls per 10-minute window (λ = 8). What is the probability of receiving exactly 5 calls in a given 10-minute window?

P(X=5) = (85 · e−8) / 5! = (32768 × 0.000335) / 120 ≈ 0.0916, about 9.2%.

Part B — Binomial

Historically, 4% of calls result in a formal complaint (p = 0.04). In a batch of 50 calls (n = 50), what is the expected number of complaints, and what distribution governs the exact count?

Expected complaints = n·p = 50 × 0.04 = 2 complaints. The exact count of complaints in 50 calls follows a Binomial(n=50, p=0.04) distribution — fixed number of trials (calls), two outcomes (complaint or not), constant probability, independent calls.

Part C — Normal (via CLT)

Average call-handling time across many agents is roughly Normal with μ = 6.2 minutes and σ = 1.4 minutes. What z-score corresponds to a call that took 9 minutes, and is that unusually long?

z = (9 − 6.2) / 1.4 ≈ 2.00. A z-score of 2 sits at roughly the 97.5th percentile — this call is notably longer than about 97–98% of calls, worth flagging as an outlier.

Notice the pattern: you didn't need three different mental frameworks, only one question each time — "is this counting successes out of fixed attempts, counting events at a rate, or measuring something continuous?" That question is the whole skill this course was built to teach.

Summary notes

  • Empirical rule: 68% within 1σ, 95% within 2σ, 99.7% within 3σ of the Normal mean.
  • Z-score: z = (x − μ) / σ — standardizes any Normal value for comparison.
  • Binomial → Normal approximation valid when n·p ≥ 5 and n·(1−p) ≥ 5.
  • Poisson is the limit of Binomial as n→∞, p→0, with n·p held constant at λ.
  • CLT: averages of samples from almost any distribution trend toward Normal as sample size grows.
